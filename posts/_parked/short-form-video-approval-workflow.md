When a video waits three days for “final approval,” the problem is often not that people reviewed it
too carefully. The team is unsure which file is final, which caption is being approved, or who can
clear the last open question.

A useful approval workflow makes those decisions explicit. It protects the work without turning each
video into a committee meeting.

## Start with one brief and one owner {#brief}

Before production or editing starts, record the job the video must do. Keep the brief short enough
for the person filming and the client approver to use:

- Audience and business goal
- One main message and the proof the video can show
- Offer or call to action, if any
- Destination accounts and planned publishing window
- Required claims, brand rules, rights, or legal review
- Brief owner and final approver

One person should own the brief and collect feedback. Other stakeholders can comment, but someone
needs authority to resolve conflicting edits. Without that owner, “feedback” becomes a collection of
unranked preferences.

## Put review gates where risk changes {#gates}

Do not ask the client to approve every punctuation change. Separate the decisions that carry real
risk:

1. **Concept:** Does the video solve the agreed brief? Is the offer and claim allowed?
2. **Final source video:** Is the edit, audio, branding, rights, and on-screen information approved?
3. **Destination details:** Are the caption, title, account, privacy, cover, and call to action
  right for each selected platform?
4. **Publish readiness:** Is the account connected, the final file attached, and the schedule
  correct?

Approval of the source video should not silently approve every destination’s metadata. Review the
changes made for each channel while keeping them attached to the same approved source. TikTok’s API,
for example, asks an app to query the creator’s current privacy options before presenting the
publish flow. That is a concrete reminder that destination choices can depend on the account, not
only on the creative brief. [See TikTok’s creator-info
requirements](https://developers.tiktok.com/docs/en/content-posting-api-reference-query-creator-info).

## Make ownership visible {#roles}

| Role | Owns | Can decide |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strategist or account lead | Brief, goal, client alignment | Scope and message direction |
| Creator or editor | Final source video and version record | Production choices within the brief |
| Reviewer | Claims, brand, rights, destination details | Approve or return with a specific change |
| Publisher | Account, schedule, and destination status | Publish when required gates are complete |
| Community owner | Replies and lead handoff | Route eligible responses to the right person |

One teammate may hold multiple roles in a small business. The important thing is that every open
decision has a named owner and next action. For client work, keep brand or account permissions
separate; “can review” should not automatically mean “can publish.”

## Close the loop after the video goes live {#loop}

Once scheduled, the video still needs a publishing status and a response owner. Confirm each
destination independently. Then capture the useful outcome: did the video reach the right audience,
prompt a question that needs follow-up, or give the team a topic worth revisiting?

A weekly client review can be simple: show what shipped, what needs attention, which conversations
were routed, and what the next video will test. That is more actionable than a long report full of
unassigned observations.
