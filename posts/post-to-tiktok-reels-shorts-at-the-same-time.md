If you make short videos and try to post them to TikTok, Reels and Shorts at the same time, you know this feeling.

You post a clip to TikTok and it does well. You're happy. So you save it, upload the same file to Instagram and YouTube, and wait for the same thing to happen.

It doesn't. The Reel sits at a fraction of the views. The Short barely moves. And you start asking the questions every creator asks at some point. Is Instagram punishing me for reposting? Was it the little TikTok logo in the corner? Or does nobody on Instagram know who I am yet?

I've asked those questions myself. I teach backend engineering through short videos on TikTok, and at MasteringBackend we publish a short every day. For a long time, getting each one onto every platform was a manual job someone on the team had to do. That's why I built Profaily.

But before building anything, I wanted to know what was true. Most of what you'll read about cross-posting is written by companies selling cross-posting tools, so it tends to skip the uncomfortable parts. I wanted the rules from the platforms themselves, and real numbers from real videos.

So I went to the sources. I read what TikTok, Instagram and YouTube publish about recommendations and reused content. I read their developer documentation, which explains a lot of the strange things scheduling tools do. And I went through one of the few public studies that compares the same video on two platforms. Here's what I found, and what I'd do with it.

## The short answer: does cross-posting hurt your reach? {#short-answer}

None of these apps posts to the others. Saving a video out of an app usually stamps a watermark on it, and both Instagram and TikTok say a watermark is a reason not to recommend a video.

But posting your own clean video to all three? That's allowed, and it's common. From what I found, how a video does on each platform depends mostly on the audience you already have there, not on the fact that it appeared somewhere else first.

So stop trying to outsmart the algorithm. Your job is simpler than that: give each platform a clean upload, and write a caption for the people who'll see it there.

## What the platforms actually say about reposting {#platform-rules}

Read the platforms' own words if you can. The details aren't what most people assume.

**Instagram.** In 2023, Instagram's head, Adam Mosseri, wrote a [long explainer on how ranking works](https://about.instagram.com/blog/announcements/instagram-ranking-explained). In it, he lists the reels Instagram shows to fewer people. The list includes "low-resolution or watermarked reels", reels that are muted or have borders, reels that are mostly text, and reels "that have already been posted on Instagram".

Instagram made the same point again in [April 2026](https://creators.instagram.com/blog/rewarding-original-creators-on-instagram): re-uploading someone else's work without adding meaningful creative input of your own isn't original, and adding a border, a watermark, subtitles or a credit doesn't change that.

Now look at the wording of the repost rule. It's about reposting on Instagram. It says nothing against a video that also lives on TikTok. The watermark rule, on the other hand, isn't limited to other people's logos. Your own TikTok watermark counts too.

**TikTok.** [TikTok's rules for the For You feed](https://www.tiktok.com/community-guidelines/en/fyf-standards) say it may not recommend "reproduced or unoriginal content that is imported or uploaded without any new or creative edits, such as content with someone else's visible watermark or superimposed logo." [TikTok's explainer on recommendations](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-us/how-tiktok-recommends-videos-for-you) also says it doesn't recommend "duplicated content".

There's one detail here I found reassuring. TikTok says videos that aren't eligible for the For You feed can still be found through search and by your followers. So even in the worst case, you're not invisible. You're just not being pushed to strangers.

**YouTube.** YouTube [treats any video "up to 3 minutes" long](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12779649) "with a square or vertical aspect ratio" as a Short. Reuse comes up in [its rules for earning money on YouTube](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392). They allow reused content when "viewers can tell that there's a meaningful difference" from the original, and they don't allow "short videos you compiled from other social media websites".

That rule is aimed at channels that repackage other people's clips. It applies "to your channel as a whole". If your channel is mostly your own work, you're not who it's written for.

When I put all three side by side, the pattern was clear. None of them cares that you cross-post. All of them care about the file.

A video saved out of TikTok carries TikTok's logo and your handle. To Instagram, that's a watermarked reel. To YouTube, a channel full of them starts to look like a compilation channel. A clean export from your editor avoids every one of those problems.

## What the numbers show about cross-posted videos {#numbers}

Rules tell you what the platforms intend. They don't tell you what happens to your views. For that I needed data, and good data on this is surprisingly hard to find.

The most transparent [study I came across](https://viral.app/blog/insights/short-form-video-trends) is from viral.app, an analytics company. It looked at 3.3 million short videos posted between October 2025 and August 2026 by about 81,000 accounts that its customers track.

I want to be fair about its limits. The accounts are mostly small. They were chosen by viral.app's customers, not picked at random. And the results show patterns, not proof. Still, it's far better than the guesses most articles pass off as facts.

The first thing it shows is that you're not alone. Among about 1,900 accounts on both TikTok and Instagram, 34% of their TikToks had a matching Reel posted within 72 hours. That's up from 27% in the period before. Cross-posting is normal now.

The second thing surprised me. Across roughly 79,000 matched pairs, the Reel got more views than the TikTok 57% of the time. Neither platform reliably wins.

The third finding is the one I'd ask you to remember. When the Instagram account had no more followers than the TikTok account, the Reel got about 0.77 times the TikTok's views. As viral.app puts it, the Reel's edge "depends on which account has more followers".

That changes how I think about the opening story. If your Reel keeps losing to your TikTok, the likeliest reason isn't a secret repost penalty. It's that your Instagram audience is smaller, or less engaged, than your TikTok one. That's slower to fix, but at least it's a problem you can work on.

A few other findings are worth knowing. Nine in ten cross-posted pairs went out within an hour of each other, so most people post everywhere at once. Typical videos were short: between 16 and 19 seconds across the three apps. Shorts over a minute got 0.81 times the views of the same channel's shorter Shorts. And the number of hashtags made almost no difference to views, which I suspect will disappoint a lot of people with a hashtag strategy.

There's one thing I couldn't find measured anywhere: how much a watermark costs you. Instagram and TikTok both state it as policy, and that's reason enough to avoid watermarks. But no published dataset I found puts a number on it, so be careful with any article that gives you one.

## Why posting to TikTok, Reels and Shorts at once is harder than it looks {#harder-than-it-looks}

If you've ever used a scheduling tool and thought, "why does it work like this?", the answer is usually in the platforms' developer rules. Reading them cleared up a lot for me, and knowing them will save you from blaming the wrong thing.

Instagram [only lets outside tools post](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/instagram-platform/content-publishing) to professional accounts, meaning Business or Creator. If your account is personal, no scheduling tool can post to it directly. You can switch in the app's settings, so this one is easy to fix. Instagram also [lists the formats it accepts](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/instagram-platform/instagram-graph-api/reference/ig-user/media) for reels, and it recommends 9:16 "to avoid cropping or blank space".

TikTok is stricter, and I think for good reasons. Every app that posts to TikTok has to pass TikTok's own review before its posts can be public. Its [developer docs](https://developers.tiktok.com/doc/content-posting-api-get-started) say plainly: "All content posted by unaudited clients will be restricted to private viewing mode." So if a tool's posts land on your TikTok as private, that's why.

Approved apps still have [rules to follow](https://developers.tiktok.com/doc/content-sharing-guidelines). They must show you a preview. You have to choose who can see the post yourself, with "no default value". And the app can only send the video after you have "expressly consent[ed] to the upload". TikTok also limits how many posts an app can make to one account in a day, typically to around 15, and it doesn't allow apps that copy "arbitrary contents from other platforms to TikTok".

YouTube is the easy one. Any channel can post a Short. If the video is vertical or square and 3 minutes or less, it counts.

But YouTube's help page on uploading Shorts carries a warning worth taking seriously: using copyrighted material without permission "could lead to you getting a Content ID claim". And on a Short [longer than a minute](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/15424877), a Content ID claim blocks the video worldwide until it's resolved.

Here's where that catches cross-posters. A trending sound from [TikTok's music library](https://www.tiktok.com/legal/page/global/commercial-music-library-user-terms/en) is licensed for use on TikTok, not on other platforms. If that same track is in the file you upload to YouTube, you may get a claim.

The fix is to use your own audio or music you have rights to, and to add trending sounds inside each app rather than baking them into the file.

## How to post one video to all three: the workflow I'd follow {#workflow}

Here's how I'd post one video to all three, with the reason behind each step.

### 1. Export a clean file, with no TikTok watermark

Don't save the video out of TikTok or Instagram. Export it from whatever editor you cut it in, so there's no logo, no handle and no app end screen. If you use CapCut, check the end of your timeline before you export, because it can add its own ending clip.

These settings work on all three apps:

- 9:16 vertical, 1080 x 1920
- MP4, with H.264 video and AAC audio
- The frame rate you filmed at, between 24 and 60 fps
- Under 3 minutes, and under a minute if Shorts matter to you
- Your own audio, or music you have rights to

One more habit worth building: keep text and faces out of the bottom fifth of the frame and away from the right edge. That's where each app puts its buttons and captions, and they're not in quite the same spot on each one.

### 2. Make sure each account can be posted to

Switch Instagram to a professional account if it isn't one already. If you use a scheduling tool for TikTok, post one test video and check that it comes out public before you trust it with anything important.

### 3. Write a caption for each audience

The video is the same, but the people watching are not. A caption is the cheapest thing to adapt, and it's exactly where most of us cut corners.

On TikTok, lead with the hook and keep it short. On Instagram, the first line has to earn the tap on "more". On YouTube, write the title the way someone would search for the topic, so people can find it.

Pick a cover frame for each app too. A frame that looks great on TikTok can get awkwardly cropped on your Instagram profile.

### 4. Decide on timing, then test it

I couldn't find any evidence that spacing your posts out helps, even though plenty of guides say it does. Both choices have good reasons behind them. Posting all at once gives you one launch moment and a simpler calendar. Spacing them out lets you reply to comments on each app while they're fresh.

My suggestion: post all three on the same day, each at the time your own analytics say your audience is most active. Do it for two weeks, then compare it with posting everything at once. Your own numbers will tell you more than any general rule, including mine.

### 5. Have someone check it before it goes live

If you post alone, a quick look is enough. Right file, right caption on the right app, audio you have rights to.

Once two or more people touch your account, though, make the check part of the process. Most of the wrong posts I've heard about happened because everyone assumed someone else had looked. The person who edited the video shouldn't be the only one who sees it before it's posted.

A simple chain works: draft, review, approve, then schedule. And agree in advance that if nobody approves it, it doesn't go out.

## A word about letting AI post for you {#ai}

AI assistants like Claude and ChatGPT can now connect to scheduling tools and prepare posts for you. It's a real help. Writing three versions of a caption, or lining up a week of posts, is exactly the kind of busywork I'm happy to hand off.

But an assistant works fast and sounds confident, even when it's wrong. It can pick the wrong clip or miss the tone of your brand, and you'd hear about it from your audience.

I found it telling that TikTok has already built a human check into its rules: a preview, a privacy choice you make yourself, and your consent before anything is posted. I'd do the same with any AI assistant. Let it draft and schedule. Keep the final yes with a person.

## What I'd want you to take away {#takeaway}

The platforms don't punish you for sharing your own video in more than one place. They punish watermarks, low quality and lazy reuploads.

So export a clean file. Check that your accounts can be posted to. Write a caption for each audience. Test your timing against your own numbers.

And if one platform keeps lagging behind, look at your audience there before you blame the algorithm. In my experience, that's where the answer usually is.

Yes. Upload the same clean file to each app yourself. It takes three uploads, but it costs nothing and each platform gets a native upload.

Not by itself. Both apps say they hold back videos with watermarks or no original edits, but neither says it penalises your own video for appearing elsewhere. In viral.app's data, how the Reel did against the TikTok depended mostly on which account had more followers.

### Why do my scheduled TikToks come out private?
